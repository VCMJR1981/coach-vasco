01Why you're stuck

More sessions won't change it. More information won't either.

You already know what a bottom turn looks like. You've watched the videos. What's missing is someone watching you and telling you which specific thing, in which specific moment, is holding the rest back.

Guidance, not answers

"Bend your knees. Look where you're going." True, generic, and impossible to act on if it isn't your actual problem.

Training the wrong thing

A late read puts you in the wrong place, and then the turn, the exit and the next move all look broken. Drill the turn and nothing changes.

No way to check

Without footage before and after, you can't tell whether you actually improved or just had a better day.

The months between trips

That time is your biggest opportunity. Surfskate and surf strength training develop the movement patterns and the physical capacity your surfing needs, so the next trip starts where the last one ended.

03The Concrete Surfers Training Method

Great surfing comes from linking each maneuver to the next, keeping the speed and the control.

A good ride isn't a list of maneuvers. It's each one feeding the next: the way a turn finishes decides how much speed and control you carry into the one after it. That connection is what we call the link, and it's what we train. When you get tired, or the wave gets bigger and faster, the maneuver itself usually still happens — what goes first is the connection between them.

Every link runs the same four phases, in order. Knowing which phase is failing is what turns "get better at surfing" into something you can actually train.

Phase 01

Read

What the wave is offering right now — the lip, the pocket, the shoulder — and choosing the moment to commit. Timing lives here.

Phase 02

Commit

You go, and you perform the maneuver. Governed by where your weight goes: in the pop-up the front foot takes around 72% of the landing load.

Phase 03

Exit

You land, or settle back onto the rail. This is where maneuvers are blown. A move isn't yours until the exit is controlled.

Phase 04

Link forward

The exit sets up the next read. Clean out, eyes already on the next section, no dead moment.

Get the read wrong and the next three phases all look broken — when none of them were the problem.

This is why we always check upstream first. A late read puts you in the wrong place, and from there the mechanics, the exit and the next turn all look wrong. Work on the read and the other three often disappear on their own. Most coaching drills the mechanics, because the mechanics are what you can see.

Six ways a link breaks

Four live in the phases above. Two sit underneath everything and degrade all four at once.

Wrong wave, wrong section, or the right section read a beat too late to use it. The fault happened before the maneuver even started. Wave selection and timing separate surfers about as much as physical fitness does. First on the list on purpose — an uncaught read problem costs you the next three answers.

You were in the right place and the movement itself was wrong. Usually weight in the wrong place: foot weighting, posture, how the rail is loaded. This is the one people assume is always the problem. Often it isn't.

Over the nose, off the tail, or straight out the back. The move existed and then the finish threw it away. Because the exit is what feeds the next read, a sloppy exit costs you more than the one turn.

Within a ride: a dead moment, a late next read, speed bled on the way out. Across a session: one error dragging down the wave after it. This is the one surfers feel most and can name least — the surfing that doesn't connect.

Not a technique problem at all. If you can't generate the force the link needs, the read comes late, the movement is weak, the exit is heavy and the recovery is slow. One cause, four symptoms. Rotational power and balance are among the clearest measured differences between intermediate and advanced surfers, which is why surf strength training isn't optional extra work — it's part of the diagnosis.

You know what to do and you're consciously checking yourself doing it. It gets worse under fear and fatigue, which in surfing is most of the time. This is why we coach with feel and external cues — "drive off the section", not "bend your knees thirty degrees". Skills built on feel survive pressure. Skills built on conscious rules break exactly when it matters.

One method, three environments

Each does something the other two can't. We're honest about where each one stops.

Surf

The real thing, and the only true test of whether anything transferred.

Its limit: access. Riding is roughly 8% of a session, so reps are scarce.

Surfskate

The movement of the link, at a volume the ocean can't give you. Hundreds of turns an hour instead of ten.

Its limit: there's no wave. It can't train reading, timing or decisions. Those come only from water.

Surf fitness

The capacity that lets your technique survive fatigue, cold and pressure. Built on stable ground, heavy and explosive.

Its limit: it builds the body, not the skill. Balance boards don't make you a better surfer.

We find what's blocking you, build the training that develops it, and re-film to check it changed.

That's a claim you can hold us to. If the second film shows nothing moved, the answer was wrong and we say so. Most coaching promises you'll feel stronger, which can't be checked and can't be failed.

06Where the method came from

It started at the oldest surf club in Portugal.

Surfing Clube de Portugal, São Pedro do Estoril. Vasco spent five years there coaching surf strength and conditioning for the competition squad. He'd started surfskating at the end of 2015, and it wasn't long before the boards found their way into the squad's training — a few athletes, a board, and a stretch of concrete next to the ocean.

In the summer of 2018 he ran a month-long surfskate competition to keep the squad training through the months they usually disappeared. Six kids at the start, twenty by the end. That summer is where Concrete Surfers began.

The following year came the first official surfskate competition in Portugal, backed by Cascais municipality. Rather than deciding it alone, he let the athletes vote on the name, the logo, the rules and the judging criteria. Forty-two turned up.

The method was built with those surfers, not written for them. Everything in it came from watching real people get stuck in the same four places — and from testing whether a change on concrete actually showed up in the water. The science came later, to check the reasoning and to mark honestly where the evidence stops and judgement begins.
